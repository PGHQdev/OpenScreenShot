What the tide pools keep when the water leaves

Twice a day the sea withdraws and leaves behind a small, complete world — sealed, patient, and busier than it looks.

By M. Okabe · 9 min read

A tide pool is an exercise in staying. The anemones fold in on themselves and wait. The hermit crabs carry their borrowed rooms from shadow to shadow. Everything that remains has agreed, in its own way, to be temporary — and to act as if it isn't.

“The pool is not a leftover of the ocean. For six hours at a time, it is the ocean — reduced, concentrated, and entirely serious.”

Walk the same stretch of shore for a season and the pools become individuals. The shallow one that warms too fast and empties by August. The deep one under the ledge that keeps a starfish like a secret. You learn them the way you learn streets in a small town.

The trick to seeing a tide pool is patience at the wrong scale. Nothing happens for ten minutes, and then everything does: a limpet lifts its shell a fraction, a whelk changes course, the surface flickers where a sculpin was pretending to be sand.

Come back at the next low tide and the cast has shuffled, the plot has advanced, and the water — the same water, more or less — has rewritten the whole stage.
